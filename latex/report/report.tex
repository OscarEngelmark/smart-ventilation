\section{Use case and building context}\label{sec:usecase}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.2; template, section 2):
% - the use case, and how it connects to BuildSim; BuildSim is not modified
% - the building and what BuildSim provides (rooms, sensors, actuators, 3D view)
% - how each process uses BuildSim
% - the actors
% - the concrete scenario the demo shows
% - why the use case matters; what makes the decision non-trivial

\textbf{The use case.} The system manages the indoor air quality of one
office room. It keeps the room's CO₂ concentration under 1000 ppm
(Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}) while running the ventilation as low as it can. CO₂ rises
as the people in the room breathe and falls as ventilation replaces room
air with outdoor air. The system therefore senses one variable, CO₂, and
acts through one device: a ventilation damper whose level runs from 0
(closed) to 1 (fully open).

\textbf{The building and BuildSim.} The room is A125, on level 0 of the
LTU A-building as modelled in BuildSim, with a floor area of 29.8 m² and
room for six people. BuildSim holds the shared state of the building in memory:
its rooms and their floor areas, the devices registered in each room with
their current values, and who is in which room. Processes read and write
that state through its REST API, and a 3D view shows the building in a
browser. BuildSim is built from the \texttt{buildingsim} folder of its
public repository~\cite{buildsim}, pinned to one commit, and never
modified. The project only adds
its own devices to it through the API: a CO₂ sensor, an occupancy sensor,
and a damper in A125.

\textbf{How the processes use BuildSim.} Five of the project's processes
talk to BuildSim. The decision service, the storage service, the
room-model service, and the dashboard never do.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The occupancy simulator and the physical model} stand in for the
  physical world (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}). The occupancy simulator writes who is in each
  room (\texttt{PUT /api/occupancy}). The physical model reads the people
  in the room, the damper's state, and the current CO₂, steps the CO₂
  forward, and writes the result as the CO₂ sensor's value
  (\texttt{PUT /api/sensors/\{id\}/value}). It is the only process that
  changes the CO₂, and its reading of the damper is what closes the loop.
\item
  \textbf{The CO₂ sensor} reads its sensor's value
  (\texttt{GET /api/sensors/\{id\}}) and publishes it to the MQTT message
  broker. Like a real sensor, it reports the room's CO₂ but does not
  produce it.
\item
  \textbf{The occupancy sensor} reads who is in the room
  (\texttt{GET /api/occupancy}), writes the head count as its own device's
  value, and publishes it to the broker.
\item
  \textbf{The damper actuator} receives the decision service's commands
  over REST and writes each new level to the damper
  (\texttt{PUT /api/actuators/\{id\}/state}). BuildSim is the one place
  the damper's position is kept.
\end{itemize}

BuildSim starts with no devices, so the CO₂ sensor, the occupancy sensor,
and the damper actuator each register their own device when they start
(\texttt{POST /api/equipment/bulk}).

\textbf{Actors.} The \textbf{operator} watches the room's CO₂, head count,
and damper level on the dashboard (Section~\ref{sec:dashboard}), which reads the stored
history rather than BuildSim. The \textbf{room's occupants} raise the CO₂
by being in the room; here they are simulated from a weekday schedule
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}). Neither is on the control path: the loop runs without anyone
acting on it.

\textbf{The scenario the demo shows.} For most of a weekday, three people
work in A125, and the damper holds them under the decision service's
950 ppm target (Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}) at about 0.4. At 13:00
a meeting fills the room to its six-person maximum for an hour. Six
people need the damper at about 0.9, most of what the ventilation can
give, so the meeting is the hardest case the decision service has to
hold under the threshold. The occupancy sensor reports six, and the
decision service raises the damper to the lowest level its room model
predicts will hold six people under the target. When the meeting ends,
the level drops back. Room time, the simulated clock all of the
project's own programs share, can run faster than the wall clock, so the
demo shows the whole
meeting in a few minutes. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports whether the threshold
holds.

\textbf{Why it matters.} The system's goals pull against each other. The
ventilation must be able to hold a full room under 1000 ppm, since a room
can fill without warning, yet for most of the day A125 holds three people
or none. Running the ventilation at the full-room setting all day keeps
the air fresh at a cost in noise and energy. A fixed low setting is quiet
but lets CO₂ pass the threshold when the meeting fills the room. The level
that meets both depends on how many people are in the room now and on how
this particular room responds to people and to ventilation. That is what
makes the decision worth automating: the system counts the people and
learns the room's response from its own stored data (Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}).

\textbf{Where the numbers come from.} \req{REG-1}'s threshold follows the
Public Health Agency of Sweden's general advice on ventilation
(FoHMFS 2014:18)~\cite{fohmfs2014}, which treats CO₂ regularly above 1000 ppm in normal
use as a sign of inadequate ventilation. It is advice rather than a
binding rule, and is stated for schools and childcare premises; Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic} covers the choice of threshold. The 2 minutes in \req{FR-2}, \req{NFR-1} and
\req{NFR-4} are the project's own figure, not taken from a source: with the damper
closed, a full room raises CO₂ by about 23 ppm a minute (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}), so
the 50 ppm between the 950 ppm target and the threshold lasts about two
minutes. A restart or a delay longer than that can let CO₂ through the
threshold before the damper opens.

\textbf{The tension between them.} \req{REG-1} pulls the damper up and \req{NFR-2}
pulls it down. The decision service meets both by choosing, on each
reading, the lowest level that still holds CO₂ under the target; \req{FR-6} is
the safety net when it has no room model to choose with.

Figure~\ref{fig:container} opens the Smart Ventilation box. Each box is
one service in \texttt{docker-compose.yml}, except the History store,
which is the Storage service's database file. The Physical model writes
the room's CO₂ to BuildSim. The CO₂ sensor reads it and publishes it to
the Message broker, as the Occupancy sensor does with the head count.
The Decision service uses both, with the room model from the Room-model
service, to choose a damper level, and sends each new level to the
Damper actuator, which sets it in BuildSim; the Physical model reads it
there on its next step. The Storage service stores the readings and
commands; the Room-model service learns the room model from them, and
the Dashboard plots them.

\section{Interfaces and data contracts}\label{sec:interfaces}

Readings, commands and the room model are published over MQTT through
the Message broker. The Decision service commands the Damper actuator
over REST, and the Storage service and BuildSim answer REST requests.
Table~\ref{tab:decisions} gives the reason for each pattern.

\section{Simulating the sensor values (the physical model)}\label{sec:simulation}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.6; template, section 6):
% - how each sensor value is produced, by a physical model per room
% - how actuator commands feed back into the model, closing the loop
% - the occupancy model: weekday schedule, meetings, empty weekends, and
%   randomness so no two days are alike
% - the governing equations
% - the approach (physics-based, data replay or hybrid), and its limitations

Two containers stand in for the physical world: the Occupancy simulator
sets who is in the room, and the Physical model computes the CO₂ that
follows (Section~\ref{sec:usecase}). The CO₂ comes from a physical model
rather than a replayed dataset, because a recorded trace cannot respond
to the damper, so the loop would not close.

\textbf{The CO₂ mass balance.} The Physical model treats the room's air as
one well-mixed volume $V$, to which people add CO₂ and from which
ventilation removes it by replacing room air with outdoor air:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:massbalance}
  V\,\frac{dC}{dt} = G\,N - Q\,(C - C_\text{out}),
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the room's CO₂ concentration, $N$ the number of people, $G$
the CO₂ one person breathes out per second, $Q$ the airflow through the
room, and $C_\text{out}$ the outdoor concentration. $C$ is a fraction of
the air (1~ppm is $10^{-6}$), and $V$, $G$ and $Q$ share one volume unit.
Every 10~s of room time ($\Delta t$), the Physical model reads $N$, the
damper level and $C$ from BuildSim, holds $N$ and the damper fixed over
the step, and writes back the exact solution
of~(\ref{eq:massbalance}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:step}
  C(t+\Delta t) = C_\text{steady}
  + \big(C(t) - C_\text{steady}\big)\,e^{-Q\,\Delta t/V},
  \qquad
  C_\text{steady} = C_\text{out} + \frac{G\,N}{Q},
\end{equation}
where $C_\text{steady}$ is the level the room settles at while $N$ and $Q$
stay as they are. Because the step is exact, its accuracy does not depend
on $\Delta t$, unlike a forward-Euler step, which is accurate only for
steps much shorter than $V/Q$. $\Delta t$ only sets how late the model
notices a change in $N$ or the damper, and 10~s is short compared with
the time scale on which the room's CO₂ changes,
$V/Q \geq V/Q_\text{max} \approx 17$~min.

\textbf{How the damper closes the loop.} The damper level
$0 \leq d \leq 1$ (0 closed, 1 fully open) sets the airflow:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:airflow}
  Q = Q_\text{min} + d\,(Q_\text{max} - Q_\text{min}).
\end{equation}
The Damper actuator writes each new $d$ to BuildSim, and the Physical
model reads it at its next step. A larger $d$ raises $Q$, which lowers
$C_\text{steady}$ in~(\ref{eq:step}) and brings $C$ to it sooner, so CO₂
falls and the CO₂ sensor's next reading shows it. $Q_\text{min}$ is the
building's background ventilation, which runs with the damper closed;
without it, CO₂ in an occupied room would rise without limit.

\textbf{Parameters.} Every value specific to the room follows from the
floor area $A$ that BuildSim gives for it, so another room needs no new
settings:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parameters}
  V = A\,h, \qquad
  N_\text{max} = \operatorname{round}\!\Big(\frac{A}{a}\Big), \qquad
  Q_\text{min} = q\,A, \qquad
  Q_\text{max} = 1.2\,\frac{G\,N_\text{max}}{C_\text{threshold} - C_\text{out}},
\end{equation}
with the symbols and values in Table~\ref{tab:simparams}.
By~(\ref{eq:step}), a full room settles exactly at the threshold when
$Q = G\,N_\text{max}/(C_\text{threshold} - C_\text{out})$. $Q_\text{max}$
is 1.2 times that airflow, so a fully open damper holds a full room under
the threshold with some reserve, while the fan stays no larger than a
full room needs. The factor 1.2 is a judgment, not a sourced figure. For
a full A125, $C_\text{steady} \approx 903$~ppm at $d = 1$, and
$C_\text{steady} = 1000$~ppm at $d \approx 0.8$.

and a full room with the damper closed gives
$dC/dt \approx 23$~ppm/min near the threshold.

\textbf{Occupancy.} Every 10~s of room time, the Occupancy simulator works
out how many people the schedule puts in the room and writes that many
to BuildSim. Half the room's capacity, rounded up, are regulars, three in
A125. They arrive one at a time at 07:00, 07:40 and 08:20, leave together
for lunch from 11:30 to 12:30, and leave one at a time at 16:00, 16:40
and 17:20. The other three come only for a meeting from 13:00 to 14:00,
which fills the room to its capacity; this is the scenario the demo
shows (Section~\ref{sec:usecase}). At weekends the room is empty. Each of
a person's times is shifted by up to $\pm$20 minutes, drawn from a random
generator seeded with the date, such that every day is different. The times are
plausible office hours, not taken from a source
or a measured building. A schedule was chosen over replaying a public
occupancy dataset, and over moving individual people along BuildSim's
walkable graph, because it shapes a scenario such as a meeting filling
the room directly.

\textbf{Limitations.} The model treats the room's air as one well-mixed
volume, so CO₂ is the same everywhere in the room, and the room exchanges
no air with other rooms. The CO₂ sensor reports the model's value
exactly, with no noise or delay. Five values are judgments rather than
sourced figures: the floor area per person, the factor 1.2, the outdoor
CO₂, $\Delta t$ and the schedule's times, and the schedule repeats the
same shape every weekday. BuildSim keeps its state only in memory, so
after BuildSim restarts, the room's CO₂ starts again from the outdoor
level. Section~\ref{sec:risks} weighs what these mean for the results.

\section{Autonomous services and data pipeline}\label{sec:autonomy}

\subsection{The autonomous service}\label{sec:decision-logic}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.7; template, section 7.1):
% - the approach, the simplest that meets the requirement, and why
% - why it fits the latency budget
% - the inputs it uses, the action it produces, how it is integrated
% - for a learned model: training, data, and the fallback when data is thin
% - how it is evaluated
% - failure and safety behavior: wrong, slow or unavailable, and the
%   fallback that keeps the room safe

The Decision service chooses the damper level. On every CO₂ reading it
predicts, with a room model learned from the stored history, how CO₂
would develop at each damper level, and picks the lowest level that keeps
it under a target. Its inputs are the latest CO₂ reading, head count and
room model, received over MQTT; its output is a damper level
$d \in [0, 1]$, sent to the Damper actuator over REST (Section~\ref{sec:interfaces}).

\textbf{Threshold and target.} CO₂ must stay under
$C_\text{threshold} = 1000$~ppm (\req{REG-1}, Section~\ref{sec:requirements}).
The plan aims lower, at $C_\text{target} = 950$~ppm, so that
a small error in the head count or the room model does not carry a room
held at the target over the threshold. With the damper closed, a full
room covers those 50~ppm in about two minutes
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation}).

\textbf{The room model.} The Decision service plans with the mass
balance~(\ref{eq:massbalance}), divided by the room's volume and with the
airflow~(\ref{eq:airflow}) put in:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:roommodel}
  \frac{dC}{dt} = a\,N - (b_0 + b_1\,d)\,(C - C_\text{out}),
  \qquad a = \frac{G}{V}, \quad b_0 = \frac{Q_\text{min}}{V}, \quad
  b_1 = \frac{Q_\text{max} - Q_\text{min}}{V}.
\end{equation}
It is never given $G$, $V$ or the airflows. The Room-model service learns
$a$, $b_0$ and $b_1$ from the stored data instead, which can determine
only these ratios. It fits them by least squares over the last 7 days of
stored readings, head counts and commands, at its start and at each
room-time midnight. Each pair of consecutive CO₂ readings $C_i$ and
$C_{i+1}$, taken $\Delta t_i$ apart, gives one equation in the three
unknowns,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fit}
  \frac{C_{i+1} - C_i}{\Delta t_i}
  = a\,N_i - (b_0 + b_1\,d_i)\,\Big(\frac{C_i + C_{i+1}}{2} - C_\text{out}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ and $d_i$ are the head count and damper level in force. A pair
more than a minute apart, or across a change in $N_i$ or $d_i$, is left
out. The rates
are published as a retained message, so a restarted Decision service gets
the last ones at once. When the history cannot tell the three rates
apart, because nobody was present or the damper never moved, the fit
gives no room model and nothing is published. Because the Decision
service learns the room rather than being told it, the same logic would
carry over to a real room that provides the same three data streams.

\textbf{The plan.} For each damper level $d \in \{0, 0.05, \dots, 0.95\}$,
the Decision service predicts CO₂ over the next hour, $0 \leq t \leq
1$~h, starting from the current reading $C_0$ and holding $d$ and the
head count $N$ counted now fixed. The prediction is the exact solution
of~(\ref{eq:roommodel}), of the same form as~(\ref{eq:step}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plan}
  C(t) = C_\text{steady}
  + \big(C_0 - C_\text{steady}\big)\,e^{-(b_0 + b_1\,d)\,t},
  \qquad
  C_\text{steady} = C_\text{out} + \frac{a\,N}{b_0 + b_1\,d}.
\end{equation}
$C(t)$ moves steadily toward $C_\text{steady}$, so it stays under a limit
for the whole hour when both $C_0$ and $C(1~\text{h})$ are under it, and
only these two are checked. The Decision
service chooses the lowest $d$ with $C(t) < C_\text{target}$ for all $t$,
and $d = 1$ when none qualifies, as for a reading
$C_0 \geq C_\text{target}$. To keep the level from flipping back and
forth at the edge between two levels, it keeps the current $d$ while that
still gives $C(t) < C_\text{target}$ for all $t$, and lowers it only to a
$d$ with $C(t) < C_\text{target} - 20$~ppm for all $t$. A command is sent only when the level changes.
In A125 this holds three people at $d \approx 0.4$ and a full meeting at $d \approx 0.9$.
 Table~\ref{tab:decisions} gives the alternatives this plan was chosen over.

\textbf{When data is thin.} Until the first room model exists, the
Decision service opens or closes the damper on the reading $C$ alone
(\req{FR-6}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:switch}
  d = \begin{cases}
    1 & \text{if } C \geq C_\text{target}, \\
    0 & \text{if } C < 800~\text{ppm}, \\
    \text{the current } d & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Besides keeping the room safe, this moves the damper between
closed and fully open, which is the history the first fit needs.

\textbf{Latency.} A command must follow its reading within 2~minutes of
room time (\req{FR-2}), and a new reading arrives every 10~s. The Decision
service predicts the hour once for the current $d$, then for
$d = 0, 0.05, \dots$ until one holds, so it makes at most 21 predictions.
Choosing the damper level for one reading thus takes at most about
0.3~µs (measured with a Go benchmark on a case that makes all 21;
\texttt{test/results/\allowbreak
bench\_planner\_2026-10-08.txt}).

\textbf{Evaluation.} \req{REG-1} checks that a full weekday stays under
1000~ppm, and \req{NFR-2} that the plan runs the damper lower over the day
than the CO₂-only switch does; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports both.

\textbf{Failure and safety.}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A wrong head count or room model.} A reading
  $C \geq C_\text{target}$ sets $d = 1$, whatever the plan predicted.
\item
  \textbf{The Decision service unavailable.} The damper keeps its last
  level in BuildSim. After a restart, the Decision service gets the last
  room model from the broker and decides again from the next reading.
\item
  \textbf{A command not applied.} When the Damper actuator answers
  anything but 200, the level is decided and sent again on the next
  reading.
\item
  \textbf{The Room-model service unavailable, or a fit refused.} The last
  room model stays in use, however old, since a room's rates change
  slowly.
\item
  \textbf{The Message broker unavailable.} No readings arrive, so no
  decisions are made and the damper keeps its last level. The Decision
  service reconnects by itself and keeps the room model it holds. The
  broker keeps the retained room model only in memory, though, so a
  Decision service that restarts after a broker restart has no room
  model and opens or closes the damper on CO₂ alone until the next fit.
\item
  \textbf{A malformed message.} The Decision service does not check the
  messages it receives against their schemas, so a CO₂ reading missing
  its \texttt{ppm} field would be read as 0~ppm, a room far below the
  target, and the damper would be lowered or closed.
  The Storage service and the Damper actuator do check theirs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The data pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.7; template, section 7.2):
% - how readings are collected, and whether the broker is a buffer
% - storage, and why
% - processing and feature engineering
% - how data is served to the autonomous service and the dashboard
% - what is retained and for how long
% - data quality: missing, stale and duplicate readings

\textbf{Collection.} The CO₂ sensor and the Occupancy sensor publish
each reading to the Message broker, and the Decision service publishes a
copy of each command the Damper actuator has applied
(Table~\ref{tab:topics}). The Storage service subscribes to all three
and writes every message to the History store. For the Storage service
the broker is a buffer: while the Storage service is stopped, the broker
holds up to 1000 messages for it, about two hours of room time, and
delivers them when it reconnects. The Decision service gets no such
backlog. After a restart it starts from the next reading, since a
backlog would make it decide on CO₂ from minutes before.

\textbf{Storage.} The History store is one SQLite file inside the
Storage service, holding every reading, head count and command
(Table~\ref{tab:decisions}). There is no separate store for recent and
for older data: every reader asks for a time window of the same tables,
and 90 room days of one room take about 120~MB. No other container
touches the file; they ask the Storage service over REST
(Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}).

\textbf{Retention.} The History store keeps the last 90 room days,
counted back from its newest timestamp. Once an hour the Storage service
deletes older rows, except each room's command in force at the cutoff,
since a damper level holds until the next command. The window is far
longer than the room model's 7 days, so recorded test days stay
available for evaluation.

\textbf{Processing and serving.} The Room-model service is the
pipeline's processing step. At each fit it asks the Storage service for
the last 7 days of CO₂ readings, head counts and commands, the commands
led by the one in force when the window starts, so the damper level is
known from the first reading. It turns them into one equation per pair
of readings~(\ref{eq:fit}) and publishes the fitted rates. When a read
fails, it tries again every 5~s of real time. The Dashboard reads the
same endpoints for the last 6~hours of room time. The Decision service
reads nothing from the History store: it takes the latest reading and
head count from the broker as they arrive, and the room model as the
retained message (Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}).

\textbf{Data quality.}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Missing.} The Room-model service's fit leaves out a pair of readings more than a
  minute apart, so a gap in the history adds no equation. Without a new
  reading the Decision service makes no decision, and the damper keeps
  its level.
\item
  \textbf{Duplicate.} At QoS~1 a message can arrive twice. The History
  store holds one row per room and timestamp and skips a second copy.
  Timestamps are whole seconds, so this assumes no source sends twice
  within one second.
\item
  \textbf{Invalid.} The Storage service checks each message against its
  schema and drops one that fails, or whose room differs from its
  topic's. The Decision service does not check
  (Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}).
\item
  \textbf{Stale.} The Decision service uses the latest head count
  however old. If the Occupancy sensor stops, a count left too low lets
  CO₂ rise until a reading at $C_\text{target}$ opens the damper fully; a
  count left too high only runs the fan harder than needed. A CO₂ sensor
  stuck at a plausible value is not detected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Process lifecycle (state diagram)}\label{sec:state}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.8; template, section 8.2):
% - a state machine for one process whose lifecycle matters
% - what happens when the network drops, and how it recovers
% - every transition is a piece of code

Figure~\ref{fig:state} shows the lifecycle of the Decision service. It
starts by connecting to the Message broker and subscribing to its three
inputs; if either fails, it exits with an error, and Docker starts it
again. Once subscribed, the Decision service opens or closes the damper
on CO₂ alone (Equation~\ref{eq:switch}) until a room model arrives, which, being retained, comes right
after subscribing unless the broker has restarted since the last fit
(Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}). From then on it plans on every
reading.

When the connection to the broker drops, no readings arrive, so no
decisions are made and the damper keeps its last level. The Decision
service tries to reconnect by itself. After each failed try it waits
twice as long before the next, up to 10~minutes (the MQTT library's
default), so a broker that returns during a wait is noticed only when
that wait ends. Once reconnected,
it subscribes again and
returns to the state it left, keeping any room model it holds.

The other processes have simpler lifecycles. One that fails to start, by registering with BuildSim or connecting
to the broker, exits and is restarted. A failed call to BuildSim in a
loop skips that cycle, and the next one tries again. The Damper actuator
instead refuses the command, which the Decision service sends again on
the next reading.

\section{Deployment and component view}\label{sec:deployment}

% Required points (report guide, sections 2.5 and 3.9; template, section 9):
% - a deployment diagram: each process and where it runs
% - latency, fault isolation, resource requirements, maintainability and
%   operational considerations
% - why each component is deployed separately, and what merging would lose
% - which components belong at the edge, which could move to external
%   compute, and why

Every process runs as a Docker container on one computer
(Figure~\ref{fig:deployment}). One \texttt{docker-compose.yml} starts all
eleven, BuildSim included, which is built from its repository at a pinned
commit~\cite{buildsim} to maintain consistent behavior. The containers 
reach each other by service name on the Compose network. The ports the
Operator, the tests and manual checks use are published to the computer,
and only on 127.0.0.1, so no other machine can reach a container.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/deployment.pdf}
\caption{Deployment diagram. Box names are those of
Figure~\ref{fig:container}, with the Compose service in brackets.
Arrows between containers are left out; they are those of
Figure~\ref{fig:container}.}
\label{fig:deployment}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Fault isolation and maintainability.} Each container is one
process with one job, and Table~\ref{tab:responsibilities} gives what
merging it into another would lose. When a container exits with an
error, Docker restarts that container alone. After a change to one
container's code, only that container is rebuilt and restarted, while the
others keep running. The History store lives
on a Docker volume, so it survives a restart or rebuild of the Storage
service.

\textbf{Resources.} With all eleven containers running, they used under
200~MiB of memory together
(\texttt{test/results/\allowbreak resources\_2026-10-09.txt}). BuildSim
and the Storage service use about 40~MiB each and every other container
about 10~MiB, rounded from one sample. No container has a resource
limit, since the computer has
memory to spare; separate processes would allow one to be set per
container.

\textbf{Latency.} All messages stay on the computer, passing through
Docker's virtual network instead of a physical one. This delay has not
been measured; it is expected to be small next to the sampling intervals
of 10~s for CO₂ and 1~min for the head count, which set how soon the
damper reacts to a change (Section~\ref{sec:dynamic}).

\textbf{Edge and external compute.} In a real building, every container
inside the Smart Ventilation boundary would run on a computer in the
building, so that the damper keeps being controlled while the internet
connection is down. The Room-model service is the only container that
could move to external compute: it fits once per room day, away from the
per-reading decisions, and if it cannot be reached the Decision service
keeps its last room model (Section~\ref{sec:state}). It stays in the
building because the fit is a small least-squares problem over seven days
of history (Equation~\ref{eq:fit}), so external compute would gain
nothing and would mean sending the history out of the building every
night.

\textbf{Where the numbers are weak.} Limits of the comparison include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The test room is easier than a real one.} Its CO₂ follows the
  same mass balance equation the room model is fitted to
  (Section~\ref{sec:decision-logic}), the head counts are exact, and the
  CO₂ sensor has no noise (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}). In a real room
  the room model would fit less well, and CO₂ would pass the target more
  often, leaving the 50~ppm margin below the threshold to absorb the
  error.
\item
  \textbf{Only one simpler method was compared.} The saving holds against
  the CO₂-only switch. A different simple method was not
  tested and might match or beat the room model.
\item
  \textbf{The saving is in damper level, not energy.} The simulation does
  not model the fan's energy use.
\item
  \textbf{The five days follow one weekday schedule,} differing only by
  each person's shift of up to $\pm$20 minutes
  (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}), so they test nearly the same day five
  times. A small spread between them is therefore expected, and says
  nothing about how the saving would change with more varied occupancy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Risks and critical reflection}\label{sec:risks}

% Required points (report guide, section 3.12; template, section 13):
% - the things that could undermine the system
% - the assumptions the design depends on

model predicts the room well. When either fails, the one safeguard left
is the full opening at the target, and that safeguard depends on CO₂
readings that are right and that keep arriving. A frozen CO₂ sensor, a
stopped Message broker and a restarted BuildSim
(Table~\ref{tab:risks}) each take that safeguard away as well.
The simulated room is also easier to predict than a real one, which
favors the room model in the comparison (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\textbf{What would be done next.} The 950~ppm target and the one-hour
horizon are untuned defaults. A target nearer 1000~ppm or a shorter
horizon would let the damper run lower, at the cost of less margin for a
wrong head count or room model, so the room model might save more than
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} shows. The room model could also be used in
more advanced ways: a plan that sets a level for every minute ahead to
use the least fan in total, rejected as harder to test and explain
(Table~\ref{tab:decisions}), or a decision policy trained in the
simulator by reinforcement learning, which learns a decision rule from
trial and error against the simulated room.
